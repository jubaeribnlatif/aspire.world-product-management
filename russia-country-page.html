01 / THE WORLD YOU'RE STEPPING INTO
Small things nobody puts in a brochure.
Not "Russia is a land of contrasts" — the specific, practical details that actually catch first-year students off guard.
"Can I just use my Bangladeshi bank card?"
Tap to reveal
Actually
No — foreign-issued Visa and Mastercard stop working the moment you cross the border. Bring cash for the first weeks and open a local MIR-network account as soon as you land.
"Do I need to register with the police right away?"
Tap to reveal
Actually
Yes — migration registration is due within 7 business days of arrival. Your university dorm usually handles it automatically; a private rental means your landlord has to file it.
"Is tipping expected anywhere?"
Tap to reveal
Actually
Only loosely, in sit-down restaurants — rounding up or leaving 5–10% is appreciated but never demanded. Cafés, shops and taxis don't expect it at all.
"Do I need to smile at strangers to be polite?"
Tap to reveal
Actually
No — a neutral, even serious, public expression is completely normal and reads as nothing more than "in public mode." It's not coldness; smiles are reserved for people you actually know.
"Is English enough to get by day-to-day?"
Tap to reveal
Actually
Not really, outside English-taught programs and big-city university areas. Learning to read Cyrillic street signs and basic survival Russian in your first term makes daily errands far less stressful.
"Is the cold really as bad as people say?"
Tap to reveal
Actually
It depends entirely on the city — Moscow winters (-10 to -15°C) are manageable with a proper coat, but Siberian cities like Novosibirsk or Tomsk routinely hit -25 to -35°C. Indoor heating is reliable everywhere, though.
02 / A YEAR IN THE LIFE
One country, three very different winters.
Tap a season — the academic year begins 1 September, and how cold it gets depends enormously on which city you land in.
Autumn
SEPTEMBER — NOVEMBER
The academic year starts on 1 September — "Day of Knowledge," marked with flowers for teachers and a first-bell ceremony. Golden, crisp weeks follow before the first snow typically arrives in most cities by November.
Pack for it: Layer up fast — September can still be mild, but temperatures drop quickly and the first proper coat purchase usually happens in October.

06 / THE RULES OF THE ROUTE
The student visa, in plain language.
Not the full legal text — the numbers and deadlines that actually decide whether you qualify.
YOUR REGISTRATION DEADLINE, DEPENDING ON WHEN YOU LAND
~7 calendar days
The 7-business-day clock runs cleanly with no weekend in the way — your host (dorm, landlord or university) should file it almost as soon as you arrive.
MIGRATION REGISTRATION: 7 BUSINESS DAYS
Weekends and public holidays don't count toward the 7 business days — landing right before one stretches the same rule across more calendar days, not fewer.
Visa fee: no consular fee for student (study) visas — the ordinary Russian student visa is issued free of charge, multiple-entry, and valid for up to a year at a time.
Before the visa: your university issues an official invitation letter once you're admitted — this is the document the visa application actually runs on, so admission has to come first.
Compulsory medical insurance: roughly ₽4,000–17,600/year depending on provider and coverage — most universities have a preferred insurer they'll point you to on arrival.
Checked against Russian consular and university international-office guidance, confirmed 26 Sep 2026
Confirmed practical detail
Foreign bank cards don't work here — plan your money before you land, not after.
Since 2022, Visa, Mastercard and similar cards issued outside Russia stop working the moment you cross the border — no payments, no ATM withdrawals. Ask your university's international office for their exact tuition-payment process before you travel (usually a bank transfer through a specific correspondent bank, sometimes cash on arrival). For everyday spending, most students bring cash for the first few weeks, then open a local MIR-network bank account once they've registered. This is purely a banking-logistics issue, not a legal barrier to studying — but it catches almost every first-time arrival off guard.
