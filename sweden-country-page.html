01 / THE WORLD YOU'RE STEPPING INTO
Small things nobody puts in a brochure.
The practical details that tend to catch first-year students off guard, not a general portrait of the country.
"What is fika, and do I have to join?"
Tap to reveal
Actually
Fika is a coffee-and-pastry break, usually mid-morning and mid-afternoon, and it is where much of the real socialising happens in labs and departments. Nobody forces you, but skipping it every time can read as distant.
"Can I get by with cash?"
Tap to reveal
Actually
Not comfortably. Sweden is largely cashless: many shops, cafés and buses only take cards or phone payments. Bring a card that works abroad for the first weeks, and expect some services to need a Swedish BankID, which comes later.
"What is a personnummer and when do I get one?"
Tap to reveal
Actually
It is Sweden's personal ID number. If you will be in Sweden for a year or more, you register with the Swedish Tax Agency after arriving. It unlocks bank accounts, BankID and subsidised public healthcare, so do it early.
"Will I find halal food outside the big cities?"
Tap to reveal
Actually
Stockholm and Gothenburg have halal butchers, grocers and restaurants in several neighbourhoods. In smaller university towns the choice is thinner, so check for nearby shops before you commit to a city.
"Is tipping expected?"
Tap to reveal
Actually
No. Service is built into the price. Rounding up the bill at a sit-down restaurant is a polite gesture, not a requirement, and cafés, taxis and bars don't expect anything extra.
"Why are people so quiet with strangers?"
Tap to reveal
Actually
Small talk with strangers is uncommon and personal space is wide, on buses and in queues. It is not unfriendliness. Friendships tend to form slowly, through repeated contact: the same lab, the same corridor, the same fika.

06 / THE RULES OF THE ROUTE
The student residence permit, in plain language.
Sweden issues a residence permit for studies rather than a visa. These are the numbers and deadlines that decide whether you qualify.
HOW MUCH YOU CAN WORK, DEPENDING ON THE TIME OF YEAR
~15 hrs / week
TERM-TIME WORK CAP: 15 HOURS A WEEK
Each square is one hour of the weekly cap. Work that is part of your education, and student-representation or research roles at your university, sit outside it. The Migration Agency also allows more work after two completed semesters in some cases, so read your own permit decision.
Permit fee: SEK 1,500 (about ৳18,500) for adults, non-refundable if rejected. You apply online, name the Swedish embassy you will visit, then give fingerprints and a photo there unless you are offered a digital passport check.
Funds to show: at least SEK 10,656 a month (about ৳131,000) for applications made in 2026. The amount is set each year, so check it for your intake.
Decision time: 75% of recent cases were decided within 3 months. The agency gives no fixed deadline, so apply as soon as your admission and tuition payment are in place.
After graduating: a permit of up to 12 months to look for work or start a business (SEK 1,500 fee) if you completed at least two semesters, passed all courses, and can show the same monthly funds. Apply before your study permit expires.
Checked against the Swedish Migration Agency's published pages, 11 Oct 2026
Rule change · 11 June 2026
Student work is now capped at about 15 hours a week during term.
For study permits granted on or after 11 June 2026, bachelor's and master's students may work about 15 hours a week, with exceptions for June to August and for work that forms part of the education. In May 2026 the government also adopted higher requirements for study results and a duty to notify the Migration Agency of your address. In practice this means part-time work cannot be your main way to cover rent, and the funds you show at application matter more. Plan your budget around savings, a scholarship or family support, and treat any job as a top-up.
