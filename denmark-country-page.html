01 / THE WORLD YOU'RE STEPPING INTO
Small things nobody puts in a brochure.
The practical details that tend to catch first-year students off guard, not a general portrait of the country.
"Do I really need a bike?"
Tap to reveal
Actually
In Copenhagen, most students end up on one: the city has separate bike lanes and cycling is the everyday way to get to class. Danish law expects a white front light and a red rear light after dark, and bike theft is common, so buy a solid lock. In smaller cities you may manage on foot or by bus.
"What are a CPR number and the yellow card?"
Tap to reveal
Actually
The CPR number is a personal ID that gives access to the Danish health service. Its yellow health card is mailed about 2–4 weeks after you apply, and you are not covered until it arrives, so non-EU students are advised to bring private cover for that gap. You cannot apply before your rental agreement starts, and your name needs to be on the mailbox.
"Can I work as much as I like?"
Tap to reveal
Actually
No, and the limit is counted per month, not per week. Your permit allows 90 hours per month from September to May and full-time in June, July and August. Going over it counts as illegal work and can lead to a warning, a fine or the loss of your permit, so keep your own record of hours.
"Is halal food easy to find?"
Tap to reveal
Actually
In Copenhagen, yes: neighbourhoods such as Nørrebro have halal butchers, grocers and restaurants. Traditional Danish food leans heavily on pork, though, so ask about ingredients in canteens and at student dinners. Smaller cities have a narrower choice, so check what is near your campus before you rent.
"Is English enough?"
Tap to reveal
Actually
For an English-taught degree, classes are fine, and many Danes speak English well. But the student residence permit requires language skills in the language your programme is taught in, and everyday admin, flat-hunting and part-time jobs go much more smoothly with some Danish. Plan to learn it.
"When do people eat dinner?"
Tap to reveal
Actually
Earlier than you may be used to. Lunch is usually around noon, often a packed lunch, and many Danish households have dinner around 18:00. In winter it is dark by about 16:00, so evenings are for candles, cooking together and staying in, the cosy mood Danes call hygge.

06 / THE RULES OF THE ROUTE
The Danish student permit, in plain language.
Denmark issues a residence permit for studies rather than a stand-alone student visa. These are the numbers and deadlines that decide whether you qualify, and one rule change you should read first.
FUNDS YOU HAVE TO SHOW (2026 LEVEL)
DKK 7,426 / month
AFTER GRADUATION: UP TO 12 MONTHS TO LOOK FOR WORK (NEW APPLICANTS FROM 1 OCT 2026)
Each square is one month of the job-seeking permit that follows a bachelor's or master's. Before 1 October 2026 it ran up to 3 years, so older guides may still show that figure. A PhD still gets 3 years.
How to apply: online through the Danish Immigration Service (SIRI, nyidanmark.dk) once you have an admission letter. Within 14 days of applying you give a facial photo and fingerprints. In Dhaka, the Denmark Visa Application Centre run by VFS Global (Delta Life Tower, Gulshan North) is commissioned by the Embassy, and a 2021 notice listed residence and study permits among what it accepts. Confirm the current biometrics booking with VFS before you plan around it.
Fee: DKK 3,060 (about ৳56,500) processing fee for the residence permit.
Funds to show: DKK 7,426 a month at the 2026 level (about ৳137,200). For a stay of more than a year you show 12 months of funds, at most DKK 89,112 (about ৳16.5L).
Who qualifies: you need an admission to a full-time programme (part-time study does not qualify) and language skills in the language of instruction. Programmes that are not state-approved give no work right and no job-seeking permit for applications made on or after 2 May 2025, so check your programme's approval before you pay a deposit.
Decision time: normally 2 months, up to 3 months if SIRI asks for more information. Your permit lapses if you give up your Danish address or spend more than 6 successive months abroad.
Working while you study: 90 hours per month from September to May, and full-time in June, July and August. Hours are counted per calendar month. Working more than that is illegal work and can mean a warning, a fine or losing your permit.
Checked against the SIRI higher-education page (updated 1 Oct 2026) and Study in Denmark, 12 Oct 2026
Rule change · 1 October 2026
New student applicants get a 1-year job-seeking permit, and cannot bring family.
From 1 October 2026, a student who finishes a bachelor's or master's (including Erasmus Mundus and Nordic Master programmes) can be granted a job-seeking permit of up to 1 year, down from up to 3 years. PhD graduates keep 3 years. The same change means a spouse, partner or children under 18 cannot be granted a permit if the student applies on or after that date; PhD families are not affected. SIRI applies the new rules by the date the application is submitted, and people who applied earlier keep the old terms. Check the SIRI page for how it applies to your application date before you plan around a long post-study stay or a family move.

07 / FUNDING THAT FITS
Scholarships worth actually applying for.
Danish funding is mostly merit-based and the number of awards is small, so treat it as a bonus rather than the plan. Work rights are capped at 90 hours a month during term, so they will not cover tuition either.

Most Danish government scholarships have no separate application: the university considers you automatically when you apply for admission. Numbers are small and differ by faculty, so apply to the programme first and read each university's own scholarship page before you budget on an award. Do not count on one when you show your funds to SIRI.
